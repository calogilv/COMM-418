# What energy does the Department of Energy use?

The Department of Energy has removed $9.5 billion of subsidies for wind and solar projects, a senior advisor of the Department [announced](https://www.energy.gov/edf/articles/letter-leadership-edf-2025-year-review-and-looking-forward-2026#_ftn1) Thursday.

The Department is planning to replace some of those investments with subsidies for natural gas and nuclear updates. As the Department of Energy reprioritizes what energy sources they want Americans to use, though, what energy sources is the Department of Energy using to keep the lights on in its own offices?

A [dataset](https://ctsedwweb.ee.doe.gov/Annual/Report/TotalPrimarySourceEnergyUseInAllEndUseSectorsByFederalAgenciesBillionBtu_NewA4.aspx?ef=Excel&fy=1&yo=&ag=8) on the Department of Energy's energy use can help to start answer that question. It has some holes and doesn't answer everything in-depth, but it serves as a start.

The dataset lists how much energy the Department of Energy has used for its facilities each year between 1975 and 2024. It categorizes energy-use by "fuel oil," "natural gas," LPG/propane," "coal," "purch. steam," "purchased renewable energy," "on-site renewables and adjustments" and "other facility energy." The data, and thus those categories, come from the Department of Energy's annual reports to Congress for the Federal Energy Management Program, which every federal department is required to report to Congress, according to the Department's [page](https://www.energy.gov/femp/federal-facility-reporting-requirements-and-performance-data) for reporting requirements and performance data. 

According to the dataset, the Department of Energy consumed over 5 trillion British thermal units of energy from natural gas didn't use any coal-created energy in 2024. The Department hasn't used coal as an energy source since 2012, according to the dataset.

That's what the dataset does show. What the dataset doesn't show is how much solar and wind power specifically the Department has used. It has two categories where it reports "On-Site Renewables And Adjustments" and "Purchased Renewable Energy," which presumably include solar and wind power, but it's not explictly clear. Those datapoints also represent a total sum of renewable energy consumption, not the distinct consumption of the many renewable energy sources that are available.
