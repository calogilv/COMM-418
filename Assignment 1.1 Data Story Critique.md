# Using age data to report politicians' driving motivators
## An NBC News article reported on why members of the silent generation continue to seek re-election when voters favor age limits 

NBC News reporters Scott Wong and Joe Murphy [reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/congress/congress-oldest-members-run-reelection-80s-rcna249479) that 13 of 24 members of Congress who are part of the silent generation are seeking re-election.

There is no specific dataset mentioned or linked, but the "Sources" notes for a graph in the article says the data comes from candidate statements, interviews, news reports and an analysis of [the @unitedstates project](https://unitedstates.github.io/), a database of information regarding legislators and legislation.

Thereby, it is possible and likely that Wong and Murphy created an original dataset to consolidate that data and did not link or reference the dataset in the article.

They combined data on silent generation members of congress with information about which of those members are planning to run for re-election.

The combination yields an [article](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/congress/congress-oldest-members-run-reelection-80s-rcna249479) that reports this Congress as the oldest Congress and reports on why silent generation members of congress are continuing to seek office. They contextualize that with a majority of voters wanting age limits for politicians. 

The big number of the story is 13. That's the number of members of Congress who are part of the silent generation and seeking re-election.

The best part of the story, in my opinion, comes from drilling into those 13. The article features quotes from nine of those 13 explaining their legislative goals and passion for politics. By using data, Wong and Murphy found people whom are at odds with voters who want younger politicians and whom journalists can explore the identities of nuances of through reporting.

Many of the interviewees said they're continuing their job because they like it, want to represent constituents and, for some, want to fight back against President Donald Trump.

With 82% of Republicans and 76% of Democrats wanting age limits according to a 2023 Pew Research Center survey, as reported by Wong and Murphy, how do older politicians' passion and joy for their job affect voters' opinions on age limits? When rallying for or against a candidate, how do different voters weigh age with passion? That's one way to expand the story.

Another way to expand the story would be to interview psychologists and cite scientific studies about the aging process and the extent to which getting older affects things like brainstorming, drafting and understanding legislation.  

Wong and Murphy primarily used words to convey the data. There is one graph showing the 24 silent generation members of Congress' current ages and what their ages would be at the end of a theoretical next term.

The graph could have been incorporated throughout the story so that readers are consistently reminded of the larger context when they're reading quotes and statements from individual Congress members. There also could be an opportunity to have a similar graph for each member of Congress, showing, perhaps, how old they were when they were first elected, how old they are now and how old they would be at the end of a hypothetical next term.

Making individual graphs for each member of Congress would help visualize the specific circumstances that member is speaking under and pair well with the story's emphasis on Congress members' individual ambitions and passions.
